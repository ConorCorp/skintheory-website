## The short answer

There's no good evidence that exercise itself causes acne. Dermatologists say things that come with it can: sweat trapped against your skin, friction from helmets, straps and tight clothes, and oily shared equipment. That advice is based mostly on clinical experience, and there are few studies testing it directly. Whey protein is a common suspect, but the evidence on it is mixed and small. Changing one thing in your gym routine at a time, and tracking it, is a reasonable way to see what applies to you.

## What the research says

The American Academy of Dermatology has a page on exactly this: [is your workout causing your acne?](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/causes/workouts) Its advice is practical. Remove makeup before you exercise. Wear clean workout clothes, washed since you last wore them, because dead skin, bacteria and oil on unwashed clothes can clog pores. Wipe off shared equipment before using it, and avoid sharing gear like helmets where you can. Pat sweat off with a clean towel instead of rubbing, and consider showering straight after your workout. If you can't, change out of your workout clothes. These are practical tips from dermatologists rather than findings from trials.

Friction is its own trigger. The AAD describes [acne mechanica](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/causes/sports-equipment), which happens when equipment or clothing traps heat and sweat against your skin and the rubbing irritates it, which can trigger breakouts in acne-prone skin. Its examples include cycling and football helmets, chin straps, shoulder pads, backpack straps, weightlifting benches and belts. It recommends moisture-wicking, loose-fitting clothing and clean, soft padding between equipment and your skin, and seeing a dermatologist if it hasn't improved after 6 to 8 weeks.

Whey protein is less clear. A [2024 case-control study of 201 young men at gyms in Jordan](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38633058/) found that 47% of those with acne had taken whey supplements in the previous three months, compared with about 28% of those without acne. That's an association, not proof: people who take whey may differ in other ways, such as diet or training. A [2024 randomised controlled trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38291989/) gave 49 men with mild to moderate acne either whey or a non-whey supplement for six months, and found no significant difference in how their acne changed. It was a small trial in men only, so it doesn't settle the question either. Put together, there's little reliable evidence either way.

## Why it varies from person to person

Where and how you train matters. Cyclists may notice spots under a helmet or chin strap, lifters where benches and belts press, and runners under headbands and caps. Some people sweat much more than others, and some fabrics trap heat more than others.

Protein is personal too. Whey is a milk protein, so if [dairy](/acne-triggers/dairy/) seems to affect you, whey might as well. Many shakes and bars also carry plenty of sugar. And starting a new training plan often comes with other changes: a new diet, different [sleep](/acne-triggers/sleep/), maybe a new body wash.

## Tips while you test

- Put a clean towel down on benches and mats, and wipe shared equipment first.
- Wash helmet pads, headbands and straps regularly, and bring a fresh shirt for each session.
- Change out of sweaty clothes soon after training, and pat sweat off rather than rubbing.
- If you take supplements, talk to a doctor or pharmacist about them, especially if you're also on acne medication.

Gym acne often overlaps with [your pillowcase and bedsheets](/acne-triggers/bedsheets/) (sweat ends up there too) and with [dairy](/acne-triggers/dairy/) if you drink a lot of milk-based shakes. If you're on a treatment, log your workouts alongside it so you know whether it's the [treatment](/acne-treatments/) or your training that's changing your skin.
