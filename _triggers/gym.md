## The short answer

Exercise doesn't cause acne, but things that come with it can. Sweat trapped against your skin, friction from helmets, straps and tight clothes, and oily shared equipment can all trigger breakouts, especially on the forehead, chin, back and shoulders. Whey protein is a common suspect, but the evidence on it is mixed. Small changes to your gym routine, and a bit of tracking, usually tell you what's going on.

## What the research says

The American Academy of Dermatology has a page on exactly this: [is your workout causing your acne?](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/causes/workouts) Its advice is practical. Remove makeup before you exercise. Wear clean workout clothes, washed since you last wore them, because dead skin, bacteria and oil on unwashed clothes can clog pores. Wipe off shared equipment before using it, and don't touch your face after handling it. Pat sweat off instead of rubbing, and consider showering straight after your workout.

Friction is its own trigger. The AAD describes [acne mechanica](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/causes/sports-equipment), which happens when equipment or clothing traps heat and sweat against your skin, with bumps often appearing where a chin strap, helmet or other gear rubs. Its examples include cycling and football helmets, chin straps, shoulder pads, weightlifting benches and belts. It recommends moisture-wicking fabric and clean, soft padding between equipment and your skin.

Whey protein is less clear. A [2024 case-control study of 201 young men at gyms in Jordan](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38633058/) found that 47% of those with acne were taking whey supplements, compared with about 28% of those without acne. That's an association, not proof. A [2024 randomised controlled trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38291989/) gave 49 men with mild to moderate acne either whey or a non-whey supplement for six months, and found no significant difference in how their acne changed. It was a small trial in men only, so it doesn't settle the question either.

## Why it varies from person to person

Where and how you train matters. Cyclists get chin-strap breakouts, climbers get chalk and rubbing on their hands and face, lifters get bench and belt friction, runners get headbands and caps. Some people sweat much more than others, and some fabrics trap heat more than others.

Protein is personal too. Whey is a milk protein, so if [dairy](/acne-triggers/dairy/) seems to affect you, whey might as well. Many shakes and bars also carry plenty of sugar. And starting a new training plan often comes with other changes: a new diet, different [sleep](/acne-triggers/sleep/), maybe a new body wash.

## Tips while you test

- Put a clean towel down on benches and mats, and wipe shared equipment first.
- Wash helmet pads, headbands and straps regularly, and bring a fresh shirt for each session.
- Change out of sweaty clothes soon after training, and pat sweat off rather than rubbing.
- If you take supplements, talk to a doctor or pharmacist about them, especially if you're also on acne medication.

Gym acne often overlaps with [your pillowcase and bedsheets](/acne-triggers/bedsheets/) (sweat ends up there too) and with [dairy](/acne-triggers/dairy/) if you drink a lot of milk-based shakes. If you're on a treatment, log your workouts alongside it so you know whether it's the [treatment](/acne-treatments/) or your training that's changing your skin.
