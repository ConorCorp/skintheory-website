## The short answer

Perioral dermatitis is a rash of small red or skin-coloured bumps, mostly around the mouth, sometimes around the nose and eyes. The British Association of Dermatologists says it's [common and mainly affects women aged 20 to 50](https://www.skinhealthinfo.org.uk/condition/peri-oral-dermatitis), though children and men get it too.

The exact cause isn't known. The clearest link is with **steroid creams used on the face**, and heavy creams and cosmetics are also suspects. It's usually treatable, but it can take weeks to months to clear and can come back. It isn't contagious.

The one-line difference from acne: it clusters around the mouth, often with dry, flaky skin, and doesn't cause blackheads, whiteheads or cysts.

## What it looks like, and how it's different from acne

The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) describes it as [small, red, acne-like bumps on light skin, and skin-coloured bumps on skin of colour](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/perioral-dermatitis). The skin is often dry and flaky, and it may itch or burn, or not bother you at all. DermNet adds that the strip of skin right next to the lips is often spared.

Cleveland Clinic says it [can look like acne and is often mistaken for it](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21458-perioral-dermatitis). Some differences, from [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/periorificial-dermatitis):

- **Acne** has blackheads and whiteheads, and can have deep lumps and cysts. These aren't typical of perioral dermatitis.
- **Rosacea** usually affects older people and a different part of the face. The British Association of Dermatologists says perioral dermatitis usually doesn't affect the cheeks and forehead.
- **Seborrheic dermatitis** tends to involve the hairline, scalp and chest.

Contact dermatitis, lip-licking rashes and impetigo can also look similar. Only a doctor can diagnose it.

This matters for you because the wrong treatment can make it worse. Putting a steroid cream on an "irritated" rash around your mouth is exactly what can keep perioral dermatitis going.

## What sets it off

**Steroid creams.** This is the trigger every source agrees on. The AAD says many people develop the rash after putting a corticosteroid on their skin for too long, including over-the-counter hydrocortisone. DermNet calls steroids the most common association, and that includes inhaled and nasal steroids, not just creams. The rash often gets better for a while each time the cream goes on, then comes back worse, which is why people get stuck in a loop.

**Heavy creams and cosmetics.** DermNet lists cosmetics, sunscreens and occlusive moisturisers, especially paraffin or petroleum-based ones. Cleveland Clinic lists heavy face creams and moisturisers. The evidence here is weaker than for steroids: it's mostly what dermatologists see in clinic, not controlled studies, and not everyone who uses rich creams gets this rash.

**Other suspects.** The AAD mentions toothpaste and allergies to things touching the skin. Cleveland Clinic lists fluorinated toothpaste, chewing gum and hormonal changes. DermNet adds hormones (the pill, pregnancy, flares before a period), and says UV light, heat and wind can make it worse. These are possible links, not proven causes, and they won't apply to everyone.

If you think a product is involved, our [skincare products guide](/acne-triggers/skincare-products/) covers how to change one thing at a time. Your [period](/acne-triggers/period/) may also be worth tracking if your flares seem monthly.

## How it's treated

SkinTheory doesn't recommend treatments and isn't a medical device. A doctor, dermatologist or pharmacist decides what's right for you. As an overview of what exists:

- **Stopping steroids on the face**, with medical help. The [AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/perioral-dermatitis) says to stop hydrocortisone you've been using on your own, but to check with the doctor who prescribed any prescription steroid before stopping it. DermNet says doctors may taper a strong cream rather than stop it suddenly, and that inhaled, nasal or oral steroids you need should usually continue. Don't stop an inhaler or tablets on your own.
- **"Zero therapy"**: stopping face creams and cosmetics and washing with water. See [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/periorificial-dermatitis) and the [British Association of Dermatologists' leaflet](https://www.skinhealthinfo.org.uk/condition/peri-oral-dermatitis).
- **Antiseptic or antibiotic creams** for milder cases. See the [British Association of Dermatologists](https://www.skinhealthinfo.org.uk/condition/peri-oral-dermatitis) and [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/periorificial-dermatitis).
- **Antibiotic tablets**, often for 2 to 3 months. See the [British Association of Dermatologists](https://www.skinhealthinfo.org.uk/condition/peri-oral-dermatitis).

Expect it to look worse before it looks better. The AAD says the rash can get worse after you stop a steroid, and that using the steroid again "will only bring temporary relief".

## When to see a doctor

Cleveland Clinic says to see a healthcare provider as soon as the rash appears, especially if it itches or burns, and to see a dermatologist if it doesn't clear after you stop steroid creams. See someone sooner if:

- you're using a prescribed steroid cream, inhaler or tablets and think it's involved. Don't stop it without asking the prescriber.
- the rash is near your eyes or your eyes are affected. Cleveland Clinic notes some people develop pink eye.
- the flare after stopping a steroid is hard to cope with. DermNet says it can be severe, and your doctor can help.

- **The skincare routine log is the key tool.** Perioral dermatitis is so tied to what you put on your face that a day-by-day record of products matters more than anything else. - **Skin feeling and mood** in every entry, for burning, tightness and how much it's getting to you.
- **A hypothesis** tested over a set number of entries, such as 21: "Does my rich night cream bring the bumps back?" Only reintroduce one product at a time, and only once your doctor agrees.
