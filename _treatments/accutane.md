## The short answer

Isotretinoin, still widely known by the old brand name Accutane (Roaccutane in the UK), is a capsule taken by mouth for severe acne. Dermatologists usually prescribe it for nodular acne that hasn't responded to other treatments.

It's prescription-only and comes with close monitoring, mainly because it can cause serious birth defects or miscarriage if taken during pregnancy. In the US, isotretinoin is only available through the FDA's iPLEDGE program, which is designed to prevent pregnancy during treatment. You'll have regular follow-ups, usually monthly, and anyone who can get pregnant has pregnancy tests before and during treatment. The FDA approved changes to iPLEDGE in 2026, so ask your dermatologist how it works for you. In the UK, you'll have regular check-ups and blood tests, and anyone who could get pregnant must use contraception from at least a month before the first dose until at least a month after the last.

A course typically lasts around 3 to 5 months, sometimes shorter or longer. The American Academy of Dermatology says clearance is often long-lasting and, for some people, permanent. DermNet notes that acne can come back months or years later for some people, who may then have another course.

## A typical timeline

Everyone's course is different, and your dermatologist will tell you what to expect for yours. As a rough picture:

- **Before you start:** pregnancy tests where they apply, blood tests, and a talk with your dermatologist about risks and benefits. This is also the best moment to take your "before" photos.
- **First weeks:** acne can get worse at the start. This early flare is a known effect and doesn't mean the medicine isn't working.
- **Throughout:** dryness is common. Red, cracked, sore lips and dry skin, eyes, mouth or nose are among the side effects listed for isotretinoin. Tell your doctor if they're severe or don't go away.
- **Mood and sexual side effects:** the NHS lists mental health problems and sexual problems, such as erectile dysfunction and low sex drive, among the possible side effects. UK prescribers are asked to check for both at follow-ups. Tell your doctor about any changes.
- **Regular follow-ups:** check-ups, blood tests where needed, and any changes your prescriber makes to your treatment.
- **End of the course:** many people see a big change, and MedlinePlus notes acne may keep improving after you finish.

Because change on isotretinoin is slow and the early weeks can look worse, the mirror is a poor judge. A daily photo record isn't.

## What’s worth tracking

- **Progress photos** at the same time and in the same light every day. Front, left and right.
- **Pimple count and how your skin feels.** Dry, tight, sore, calm. Over months, this shows the trend.
- **Side effects you notice,** like dry lips, dry or flaky skin and irritation, so you can tell your dermatologist exactly when they started.
- **Mood.** The drug label warns that isotretinoin may cause depression, psychosis and thoughts of self-harm. A 2024 review of 25 studies with over 1.6 million people found no increased risk of suicide or psychiatric conditions across whole populations, but its authors still advise doctors to watch for mental distress, because one person can react differently from the average. Any mood changes, low mood or thoughts of self-harm should be reported to your doctor straight away. A daily mood log is for bringing to your doctor, not for judging it yourself.
- **Dates:** when you started, and any changes your prescriber makes.
- **Your routine around it:** moisturiser, lip balm, sunscreen, cleanser.
